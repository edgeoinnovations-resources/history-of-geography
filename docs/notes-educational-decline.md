# Research Notes: The Minimization and Decline of Geography in Education (primarily U.S., mid-20th century onward)

Dense, citation-keyed notes for the report-writer agent. Inline keys like [Smith 1987] resolve in the References section. Short quotes (<20 words) are in quotation marks with attribution. Claims that could not be fully verified are explicitly flagged.

---

## 1. Summary timeline

- 1916 — NEA Committee on Social Studies report institutionalizes "social studies" as a fused K-12 field containing geography [NEA 1916; Nelson 1990].
- 1918 — Cardinal Principles report; per Diane Ravitch, "Neither history or geography survived as a subject; both were submerged into the new field of the 'social studies'" [Fallace 2008, quoting Ravitch].
- 1939 — Hartshorne's *The Nature of Geography* codifies geography as areal differentiation / regional description (the later "idiographic" image) [Hartshorne 1939].
- 1948 — Harvard eliminates geography; President Conant reportedly concludes "geography is not a university subject" [Smith 1987].
- 1953 — Schaefer's "Exceptionalism in Geography" attacks the Hartshornian regional paradigm as unscientific, opening the quantitative revolution [Schaefer 1953].
- 1963 — University of Pennsylvania geography department closed; 1967 — Yale's ends [Sacks 2015; Zhang 2024].
- mid-1970s — only ~9% of U.S. students in grades 7–12 enrolled in a geography course [West 1992].
- 1982 — University of Michigan department discontinued (Regents vote 19 June 1981, effective 30 June 1982) [Huntley & Rosenblum 2021].
- 1986 — Columbia's department dissolved; University of Chicago's department (the first in the U.S., 1903) replaced by a "Committee on Geographic Studies" [Sacks 2015; UChicago Library n.d.].
- 1984–1994 — reform wave: *Guidelines for Geographic Education* (five themes, 1984), GENIP (1985), NGS Alliance Network (1986), *Geography for Life* standards (1994) [GESP 1994; Boehm et al.].
- 1988–2006 — National Geographic Gallup/Roper surveys repeatedly document U.S. geographic illiteracy; U.S. young adults rank last (1988) or second-to-last (2002) internationally [EdWeek 1988; CBS 2002; NGS-Roper 2006].
- 1994–2018 — NAEP geography: flat, low proficiency; 2018 grade-8 average statistically unchanged from 1994; only ~25% at/above Proficient [NCES 2020; NAGB 2020].
- 2001 — NCLB names geography one of nine "core academic subjects," but it is the only one given no dedicated federal funding stream [ESRI 2004; AAG/ESRI 2015; Kachina 2011].
- 2019 — National Assessment Governing Board eliminates geography (with economics, arts, foreign-language expansion) from the NAEP schedule through ~2029/2030 [Solem & Stoltman 2020; EdWeek 2019].
- 2010s (UK contrast) — England's EBacc accountability measure (history-or-geography slot) drives GCSE geography uptake from 26% of pupils (2010) to 36% (2014); RGS reports recovery after an early-2000s dip [DfE 2014; RGS n.d.].

## 2. Harvard, 1948: the "academic war over the field of geography"

- Canonical source: Neil Smith (1987), *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* 77(2): 155–172, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8306.1987.tb00151.x (bibliographic details verified via CrossRef) [Smith 1987].
- Smith's abstract: after "modest but optimistic expansion in the 1940s," Harvard's geography program "was suddenly terminated in 1948," triggering a widely publicized "academic war over the field of geography" (contemporary press phrase that Smith adopts as his title) [Smith 1987].
- The blow was double: Harvard's prestige, plus the fact that in the course of the closure the president of Harvard suggested geography was not an appropriate university subject [Smith 1987, abstract].
- **Conant quote provenance**: the famous line — Conant declaring that "geography is not a university subject" — is reported in Smith's archival reconstruction (Smith 1987, p. 159, as cited by the RGS-IBG Geography Directions summary [Sacks 2015]). It derives from Smith's archival work (Harvard and Johns Hopkins archives, Isaiah Bowman's papers, plus dozens of interviews), not from a published Conant statement; it should be presented as reported/secondhand, with Smith 1987 as the authority. James B. Conant was Harvard president 1933–1953.
- **Isaiah Bowman's role**: Smith's central revisionist finding — Bowman (Johns Hopkins president, former AGS director, the most politically powerful American geographer of his era) "appears to have been more instrumental than is generally realized" in the termination [Smith 1987, abstract]. Bowman advised Conant; his disdain for Harvard's program (and for department figure Derwent Whittlesey) shaped the outcome. Smith's obituarists note he found in Bowman's papers "rich insight into the closing of Harvard's geography department in 1948" [Maxwell obituary 2012].
- **Whittlesey and homophobia**: multiple accounts (building on Smith's research) report that the rumored homosexuality of department chair Derwent Whittlesey (and his partner Harold Kemp's promotion case) was a complicating factor; Bowman's hostility had a homophobic edge [Maxwell obituary 2012; Rose-Redwood et al. 2012 refs; Zhang 2024]. Key words of Smith's own article include "Derwent Whittlesey" [Smith 1987].
- **Proximate institutional trigger**: a dispute over the promotion of geographers within the Division of Geological Sciences; geologist Marland P. Billings wrote letters questioning human geography's importance and arguing resources were better spent on geology — these letters set off the administrative review that ended the department in 1948, with faculty reassigned or retired [Wikipedia-Billings, citing Smith 1987].
- Context factors cited in later accounts: postwar financial strain, an ad hoc committee unable to define geography's core, Red Scare-era anxieties [Zhang 2024].
- Contemporary reaction: geographers on both sides of the Atlantic criticized the decision; the Harvard Crimson editorialized in 1951 ("Geography Razed") that the university had "emasculated its geography department, quickly, silently," and urged its resurrection; the affiliated Institute of Geographical Exploration (privately funded by Alexander Hamilton Rice) was closed in 1951 [Crimson 1951].
- Jean Gottmann (1982) called the closure "a terrible blow" from which American geography "has never completely recovered" (quoted in Smith's account and subsequent summaries) [Sacks 2015; Zhang 2024].
- Long-run significance: Smith argued the episode raised "a larger question concerning the vulnerability of geography, at Harvard and elsewhere" — why geography was "so vulnerable in the first place" (Smith 1987, 167) [Smith 1987; Maxwell obituary 2012].
- Harvard did not grant another geography degree for ~70 years (a 2018+ graduate described as the first in seven decades) [Zhang 2024].

## 3. The cascade of departmental closures

- Pattern: Harvard 1948 → other elite private universities. "Other American private institutions soon followed Harvard's decision" [Sacks 2015].
  - **Pennsylvania**: department merged into Wharton, closed 1963 [Sacks 2015; Zhang 2024].
  - **Yale**: program lingered "near death" until 1967 [Sacks 2015; Zhang 2024]. (Yale had been home of Ellsworth Huntington, the arch environmental determinist — see §4.)
  - **Michigan**: discontinued 1982. Board of Regents voted unanimously 19 June 1981, effective 30 June 1982, after a multi-year review (1975–1982). VP for Academic Affairs Billy Frye cited economic climate, deficiencies in scholarly quality/productivity, and "lack of centrality" to the College's curriculum. Huntley & Rosenblum show administrators facing austerity asked which disciplines were "least essential," and "many at Michigan had been prepared to answer 'geography' since at least the mid-1970s." They call for a "breakdown historiography" of the discipline's deinstitutionalization. Michigan was "the first in a series of major department closures in the early to mid-1980s" [Huntley & Rosenblum 2021; UMich Bentley finding aid].
  - **Chicago**: the first geography department established at any American university (coursework from 1902–03; builders Rollin D. Salisbury and J. Paul Goode); ranked #1 in the 1925 Hughes ranking of PhD-granting departments; after retirements it was "succeeded by the Committee on Geographic Studies in 1986" — i.e., demoted from department status [UChicago Library n.d.; Warf & Webster 1980].
  - **Columbia**: "easily the most prodigious of the Ivy League," ended 1986 for lack of enrollment and funding [Sacks 2015].
  - **Northwestern**: had no independent department before 1945, then rose rapidly to prominence by 1965 [Lukinbeal et al. 2026]; the department was later disbanded (commonly dated to 1986-87) — **precise closure date not verified against a scholarly source in this research pass; treat the 1986 date as plausible but unconfirmed**. Student journalism confirms the department's effective disappearance (a single remaining professor for decades) [WNUR 2022].
  - **Stanford**: the task brief lists Stanford among closures; **no reliable source verifying a Stanford geography department closure was found — flag as unverified/likely conflation** (Stanford never built a major 20th-century geography department).
- Aggregate effect: top-8 (1925) departments at Harvard, Yale, Columbia, Clark either disappeared or declined sharply in later rankings [Warf & Webster 1980]. Geography survived mainly at public and land-grant universities [Zhang 2024].
- Cyclical-crisis diagnosis: weak K-12 geography → few undergraduates choosing geography → low enrollments used to justify closures → further invisibility [Sacks 2015].

## 4. Why did American geography decline? (intellectual + institutional causes)

- **Environmental determinism's rise and fall**: by the 1910s–20s environmental determinism was the dominant paradigm of U.S. geography; leading theorists Ellen Churchill Semple and Ellsworth Huntington (Yale) [GeoCurrents 2011; Gallaher & Shirlow 2009 as quoted]. Huntington mapped "climatic energy" peaking conveniently around his own New Haven; his *Character of Races* (1924) tied "ethnic constitution" to climate [Livingstone 1992 as quoted; GeoCurrents 2011]. The paradigm was racist in content (Semple: tropics "kept him a child") [Wikipedia-Semple]. Franz Boas's cultural anthropology was in part a direct rebuttal [GeoCurrents 2011]. By mid-century determinism was "discredited and marginalised within mainstream academic geography" [Hulme 2011]; the embarrassment tainted the whole discipline's scientific standing and left it "long plagued" by the association [Farbotko 2025].
- **The idiographic/regional image**: Hartshorne's *The Nature of Geography* (1939, published in Annals vol. 29) defined geography as areal differentiation — description of unique regions rather than law-seeking science [Hartshorne 1939]. In the late 1940s–early 1950s "the regional tradition... dominated geography studies," coinciding with department closures [Wikipedia-QuantRev]. Schaefer (1953) attacked this "exceptionalism" as methodologically indefensible [Schaefer 1953] — but the quantitative/scientific turn came too late for Harvard, and a discipline seen as descriptive, synthetic, and non-rigorous was institutionally defenseless when austerity or skeptical presidents arrived [Smith 1987; Huntley & Rosenblum 2021].
- **Perceived lack of rigor / ill-defined core**: at Harvard an ad hoc committee could not agree on what geography's core was [Zhang 2024]; at Michigan, administrators judged the department deficient in "overall department scholarly quality, productivity, and lack of centrality" [UMich Bentley finding aid]. Huntley & Rosenblum emphasize the gap between the discipline's "unity myth" self-defense and its actual practices [Huntley & Rosenblum 2021].
- **Personal/institutional politics**: Bowman's interventions (Harvard); Billings's geology-vs-geography resource fight (Harvard); homophobia around Whittlesey (Harvard); local austerity politics (Michigan 1970s–80s) [Smith 1987; Wikipedia-Billings; Huntley & Rosenblum 2021].
- **Paradox**: the collapse happened precisely as U.S. global engagement (WWII, Cold War) made knowledge of "international studies, lands, and peoples" more important, and while geography thrived in Britain [Sacks 2015].

## 5. K-12: absorption into "social studies"

- The term "social studies" as the umbrella for school subjects dealing with human relationships "dates from 1916": the Report of the Committee on Social Studies, Commission on the Reorganization of Secondary Education, NEA — published as U.S. Bureau of Education Bulletin No. 28, *The Social Studies in Secondary Education* (63 pages) [NEA 1916; AHA n.d.; Nelson 1990].
- The committee (chaired context: CRSE chartered 1913 under Clarence Kingsley; social-studies subcommittee led by Thomas Jesse Jones) framed the field around "good citizenship" rather than disciplinary knowledge [NCSS 1995].
- The 1916 plan listed geography within the junior-cycle (grades VII–IX) social studies sequence alongside European history, American history, civics — i.e., geography became one strand inside a fused field rather than a standalone discipline [NEA 1916].
- The National Council for the Social Studies (founded 1921) standardized the term [AHA n.d.].
- Critics' assessment: Ravitch and others identify the 1916 report as the turning point where the history-centered (and geography-containing) course sequence was replaced by "utilitarian, anti-intellectual, trans-disciplinary social studies"; Ravitch on the 1918 Cardinal Principles: "Neither history or geography survived as a subject; both were submerged into the new field of the 'social studies'" [Fallace 2008, quoting Ravitch 2003; Graham as quoted therein]. (Note: Fallace and Woyshner caution that the "struggle metaphor" overstates suddenness — the shift unfolded over the 1920s–1940s with "fusion" courses [Fallace 2008].)
- Quantitative outcome: by the mid-1970s, the share of U.S. 7th–12th graders enrolled in a geography course had fallen to ~9% (national survey cited by Education Week) [West 1992].
- By 2006, 42 states did not include geography in their high-school curriculum (National Geographic Education Foundation figure) [Kachina 2011]. By 2015, only 17 states required a stand-alone geography course in middle school and only 10 in high school (AAG figures) [AAG/ESRI 2015].

## 6. Documented geographic illiteracy

### National Geographic surveys (Gallup 1988/1989; Roper 2002; Roper 2006)
- **1988 (Gallup for NGS)**: 10,820 adults in 9 nations; respondents located 16 features (13 countries + Central America, Pacific Ocean, Persian Gulf) on an unmarked world map. Swedes best (11.6/16); Americans 8.6/16, ahead of only Italy and Mexico. U.S. 18–24-year-olds averaged 6.9/16 — "fewer than the average of any other age group in any country." The U.S. was the only country where the youngest did *worse* than the oldest cohort, and young adults scored lower than in a comparable 1947 survey [EdWeek 1988]. A 1989 Soviet extension found young Soviets (9.3/16) well ahead of young Americans; Gallup: Americans and Soviets showed "an astonishing lack of awareness of the world around them" [Deseret 1989].
- **2002 (RoperASW, NGS)**: ~300+ per country, ages 18–24, nine countries, 56 questions. U.S. average 23/56 — a "D"; second-to-last, ahead of only Mexico (21); Sweden best (40). Only 13% of young Americans could find Iraq on a map (on the eve of the war); 17% could find Afghanistan (83–87% failure rates on those items); only 51% could find New York State; 11% could not find the U.S. itself [CBS 2002; AP 2002; EdWeek 2002]. Also notable: 55% said they had taken a school geography course, up from 30% in 1988 [EdWeek 2002].
- **2006 (Roper Public Affairs, NGS)**: 510 Americans ages 18–24, surveyed Dec 2005–Jan 2006. Despite three years of war, 63% could not find Iraq on a Middle East map; 88% could not find Afghanistan on an Asia map; 75% could not locate Israel (and Iran); 47% could not find the Indian subcontinent; 33% could not find Louisiana months after Katrina; 50% could not find New York State; ~6 in 10 could not locate Ohio; 74% named English (not Mandarin) the world's most-spoken native language; two-thirds didn't know the Oct 2005 earthquake was in Pakistan; overall score ~54% correct. Fewer than 30% thought knowing news-relevant country locations important; 14% thought another language necessary. Report conclusion: young Americans "are unprepared for an increasingly global future." Release accompanied launch of NGS's "My Wonderful World" campaign; NGS CEO John Fahey: "Geographic illiteracy impacts our economic well-being, our relationships with other nations and the environment" [NGS-Roper 2006; CNN 2006; NBC 2006; Sun Journal 2006; ESRI 2009; SAGE ency.].
- Caveat for the report-writer: geographic ignorance is not uniquely American — Saarinen & MacCabe's 1995 sketch-map study put U.S. university students near the global average [SAGE ency.] — but the U.S. consistently ranks at/near the bottom of wealthy countries in these polls.
- Earlier local alarm: 1983 Dallas Times-Herald survey — >20% of Dallas elementary pupils could not locate the U.S. on a world map [West 1992].

### NAEP (The Nation's Report Card)
- Trial geography assessment 1988 (HS seniors averaged 57% correct on 67 multiple-choice items) [West 1992].
- Full NAEP Geography administered 1994, 2001, 2010, 2014, 2018 (grade 8 series; earlier rounds also tested grades 4 and 12; the 2018 round was grade-8 only after a 2015 NAGB schedule cut removed grade 12) [NCES n.d.; NAGB 2015].
- 2014: 27% of 8th-graders at/above Proficient in geography [NCES 2015]. 2018: average score down 3 points vs 2014; declines concentrated among lower-performing students; 25% at/above Proficient; **2018 average not significantly different from 1994** — a quarter-century of flat, low achievement [NCES 2020; NAGB 2020].
- July 2019: NAGB's updated 10-year NAEP schedule **eliminated geography** (plus economics, arts, and planned foreign-language expansion) from the testing calendar through ~2029/2030, citing budget constraints (only reading and math are congressionally mandated). NCSS called it "a devastating blow to our students"; NCSS president Tina Heafner: "We were completely blindsided." So the main national instrument measuring geography learning no longer exists [Solem & Stoltman 2020; EdWeek 2019; NCSS 2019; K12Dive 2020].

## 7. Policy marginalization: named "core," funded at zero

- 1989 Charlottesville Education Summit (G.H.W. Bush + governors) put geography among the national-goals core subjects; carried into Goals 2000 / Improving America's Schools Act under Clinton [NGS blog 2008; ERIC ED418040].
- NCLB (2001) listed nine "core academic subjects": English/reading/language arts, mathematics, science, foreign languages, civics and government, economics, arts, history, geography. Geography was "the only discipline identified in the act with absolutely no specific program provisions or funding associated with it" — e.g., FY2004 federal history-education funding ~$100M vs. $0 for geography [ESRI 2004; AEI 2021; Kachina 2011; NGS blog 2008].
- Consequence (as argued in the geography-education literature): with testing and funding focused elsewhere, "geography as a separate subject in the majority of public schools around the country ceased to exist" [Kachina 2011].
- ESSA-era advocacy: AAG spent a decade lobbying for a dedicated K-12 geography/GIS funding stream in ESEA reauthorization [AAG/ESRI 2015].

## 8. Responses and reform attempts (1980s–2010s)

- *Guidelines for Geographic Education: Elementary and Secondary Schools* (1984), Joint Committee on Geographic Education of the AAG and NCGE — introduced the **Five Fundamental Themes**: Location, Place, Human-Environment Interaction (relationships within places), Movement, Regions. Widely credited with "ushering in the modern period of geography education" [JCGE 1984; ERIC ED252453; Boehm et al.].
- **GENIP** (Geographic Education National Implementation Project), 1985 — AAG/NCGE/NGS/AGS consortium to implement the Guidelines [Vender 2003; Boehm et al.].
- **NGS Geographic Alliance Network**, from 1986 — state-level teacher alliances for in-service training, eventually in ~all states [Boehm et al.; Dulli & Goodman 1994 as cited].
- **Geography Awareness Week** (from 1987) and the **National Geography Bee** (first held 1989, created "as a response to rising geographic illiteracy") [AEI 2021].
- **NAEP Geography Framework** (NAGB 1992) → first full NAEP geography assessment 1994 [Boehm et al.].
- ***Geography for Life: National Geography Standards 1994*** — Geography Education Standards Project (AGS, AAG, NCGE, NGS): 18 voluntary standards benchmarked at grades 4/8/12, organized under Six Essential Elements (World in Spatial Terms; Places and Regions; Physical Systems; Human Systems; Environment and Society; Uses of Geography); goal: the "geographically informed person" [GESP 1994; ERIC ED381480; Vender 2003]. 2nd edition 2012 (Heffron & Downs) [Womac].
- **My Wonderful World** campaign (NGS, 2006) to enlist business/nonprofits/educators after the 2006 Roper results [NBC 2006; CNN 2006].
- *Road Map for 21st Century Geography Education* (2012) — later landmark [Springer-GeoEd].
- Net assessment: these efforts rebuilt frameworks and teacher networks but — given NAEP stagnation (§6) and zero federal funding (§7) — did not restore geography as a standalone school subject in most states.

## 9. International contrast

### England/UK — stronger institutional position, own dip and recovery
- Geography became a **foundation subject in England's National Curriculum** (from the 1988 Education Reform Act), compulsory to age 14; it is an independent subject in English schooling rather than fused into social studies [Hall et al. 2015 context; EU curricula study].
- Early 2000s: "entry levels for geography GCSE (and A Level) experienced significant decline during the early 2000s for reasons associated with the school targets existing at that time. Since 2010 the numbers... have recovered" [RGS n.d.]. Over 1994–2014, GCSE geography entries fell ~15% [Smithers 2014].
- **EBacc effect**: the English Baccalaureate accountability measure (introduced 2010/11) requires GCSE passes in English, maths, two sciences, a language, and **history or geography** — restoring a hard incentive. Share of pupils taking GCSE geography rose from 26% (2010) to 36% (2014) [DfE 2014; Campaign for the Arts 2025 (EBacc definition)].
- A-level geography is recognized by the Russell Group as a "facilitating subject" [RGS n.d., citing Informed Choices 2011]. (A-level entries have fluctuated — e.g., an 11.3% one-year drop in 2018 amid exam reform [TES 2018] — but geography's structural position in English schooling remains far stronger than in the U.S.)
- UK higher education: geography remained a major university discipline (Oxford, Cambridge, LSE etc. kept departments throughout the period when U.S. elites were closing theirs) [Sacks 2015 implied: Conant "ignored British investment in the discipline"]. British departments have nonetheless seen merger/reclassification pressures since the mid-1990s [Hall et al. 2015 via Sacks 2015].

### Germany and continental Europe
- Geography (Erdkunde/Geographie) is an **independent, generally compulsory school subject** in Germany (as in Slovenia, Hungary, Norway, Netherlands, Scotland, England), not an element of a fused social-studies block [EU curricula study].
- Germany's geography community (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Geographie) produced its own national education standards, "making geography the first school subject with national standards outside the KMK" (the states' standing conference) [Bagoly-Simó/ERIC EJ1157788].
- (Coverage of Germany and other countries here is thinner than for the UK — see deviations.)

### Comparative judgment
- The U.S. is distinctive in (a) having folded geography into social studies in 1916, (b) having purged it from elite universities 1948–1986, and (c) funding it at zero within federal K-12 policy — whereas the UK and Germany retained geography as a named, examined school subject and a prestige university discipline. The international survey gap (§6) is the measurable correlate.

## Notes on verification and disputed points

1. **Conant quote**: reported via Smith's 1987 archival reconstruction (p. 159); no primary published Conant source; present as "reportedly" [Smith 1987; Sacks 2015].
2. **Stanford closure**: unverified; recommend omitting Stanford from any closure list.
3. **Northwestern 1986**: closure real but precise date unverified in scholarly sources consulted; say "mid-1980s."
4. **"Harvard's was the first domino"** framing is standard but Penn/Yale closures had local causes too; Huntley & Rosenblum caution these histories are under-researched outside Harvard [Huntley & Rosenblum 2021].
5. 2002 survey "87% cannot find Iraq" (AP wording) vs 2006 "63% could not find Iraq": different map tasks; both verified; don't conflate years.
6. All CrossRef-verified DOIs: Smith 1987 (10.1111/j.1467-8306.1987.tb00151.x); Huntley & Rosenblum (10.1080/24694452.2020.1760780); Schaefer 1953 (10.1080/00045605309352114).
